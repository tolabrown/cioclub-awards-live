A NOTE TO THE READER
This book started as research. But somewhere along the way, it became something more.
I did not arrive at this topic by accident. I went looking for it. For years, I carried questions that never fully settled. I watched stories of domestic violence appear briefly in public conversation and then disappear. A woman would die. There would be outrage. There would be prayers. And then, slowly, silence would return. I kept wondering what happened in the space between those headlines.
In 2022, when gospel singer Osinachi Nwachukwu¹ died, the public response followed a pattern Nigerians have seen before. First came the denial. Her death was explained as illness. Then the truth emerged slowly: years of physical, emotional, and psychological abuse. Her husband was eventually convicted, and the case was called a landmark moment of justice. But beneath the outrage lay a quieter, more uncomfortable question: why does suffering so often have to become fatal before it is believed?
Tanitoluwa Adeniba, Ph.D.
I grew up in Nigeria, where marriage is honored, family is sacred, and endurance is often praised as a virtue. I listened as women were advised to be patient, protect the home, and avoid public embarrassment. I noticed how sympathy and judgment could coexist — a woman might be pitied for her pain and blamed for staying in the same breath. Over time, I became increasingly unsettled by the way suffering could be normalized in the name of love, faith, or tradition. As a researcher, I knew that one way to confront what troubled me was to study it carefully. Not to sensationalize or simplify it, but to name it clearly.
So, I did.
One hundred and one Nigerian mothers helped me understand how domestic violence shapes the relationship between a mother and her child. The women who participated came from different regions of Nigeria and from a wide range of social and economic backgrounds. Some were married. Others were separated or divorced. Many were in their late thirties and early forties, with children still living with them at home. Building trust was essential because domestic violence remains difficult to discuss openly in many Nigerian settings. Some women responded cautiously at first. Others spoke with a sense of relief that someone was finally asking these questions out loud.
The women named in this book are composite figures, their stories drawn from patterns that emerged across many lives. Details have been blended, altered, or omitted to protect privacy. What remains are the emotional, relational, and cultural truths that surfaced repeatedly, truths shaped
THE HO US E W A S NEVER Q UIET 
by the resilience of the women who trusted this research, and by the courage it took to speak at all.
I set out expecting patterns, numbers, and responses I could organize into charts and categories. What I did not expect was how much lived between the lines. Behind every statistic was a woman navigating fear, culture, motherhood, faith, and expectation all at once. Some answers were straightforward. Others felt softened and cautious, almost protective of something unnamed. Sometimes what stayed with me most was not what was written, but what felt too heavy to say outright.
The House Was Never Quiet is not just a collection of testimonies. It is an effort to make sense of patterns that surfaced across many lives. Although the stories focus on women, the subject is not solely about women. Violence rarely exists in isolation. It survives inside systems, inside families that protect silence, communities that hesitate to intervene, and institutions that struggle to recognize abuse when it does not look dramatic. Religious spaces, cultural traditions, and social expectations all influence how violence is understood, explained, or minimized.
I did not write this book to condemn culture or discredit tradition. I am shaped by the same cultural and religious traditions I examine here. But love for culture does not require silence about the abuse that can occur within it. In fact, love requires honesty.
If you are reading this as someone who has personally experienced abuse, I hope these pages help name things that may have once felt confusing or invisible. If you have
Tanitoluwa Adeniba, Ph.D.
never experienced domestic violence directly, this work still belongs to you. It asks what we have overlooked, what we have explained away as normal, and whether we listened closely enough when someone hinted at pain.
I wrote this book to name what often goes unnamed. To widen the lens around what we call normal. And to invite a more honest conversation about what safety, love, and responsibility should look like inside our homes.
Because what happens inside the house shapes lives far beyond its walls. And the house is rarely as quiet as it appears.
Tanitoluwa Adeniba, Ph.D.

FOREWORD
Domestic violence is often spoken about in hushed tones—if it is spoken about at all. For many, it is perceived only in its most visible and extreme forms, yet the reality we encounter daily at the Lagos State Domestic and Sexual Violence Agency (DSVA) tells a far more complex story. Violence within the home is frequently quiet, deeply embedded in cultural norms, and sustained by silence, stigma, and long-held beliefs about family, gender, and endurance. It is within this context that The House Was Never Quiet emerges as both a necessary and courageous contribution to the ongoing conversation about domestic violence in Nigeria.
This book does not merely recount stories of abuse; it interrogates the systems that allow such abuse to persist. Drawing from research and lived experiences, it reveals how domestic violence is often normalized within Nigerian society, hidden behind cultural expectations of patience, respect, and marital duty. The narratives presented challenge us to rethink what we define as violence, particularly forms
Tanitoluwa Adeniba, Ph.D.
such as coercive control, emotional abuse, and financial manipulation, which often leave no visible scars but inflict profound psychological and social harm.
One of the most striking insights from this work is the reality that many women do not identify their experiences as abuse. This is not due to a lack of awareness alone, but rather the result of deeply ingrained cultural scripts that equate endurance with virtue and silence with strength. From a young age, many Nigerian girls are socialized to prioritize family preservation over personal safety, to protect the image of marriage at all costs, and to internalize suffering as a necessary component of womanhood. This book courageously disrupts these narratives.
Equally important is its focus on mothers and the often-overlooked impact of domestic violence on parenting and child development. It highlights how the stress of living in abusive environments affects not only women but also the children who grow up within these spaces. Even when violence is not directly witnessed, its presence is felt through tension, fear, and emotional withdrawal. These experiences shape how children understand relationships, conflict, and love, often perpetuating cycles of silence and harm across generations.
In Nigeria, addressing domestic violence requires more than individual interventions; it demands systemic and cultural transformation. Institutions, communities, faith leaders, and families all have critical roles to play. At the Lagos State Domestic and Sexual Violence Agency, I have
THE HO US E W A S NEVER Q UIET 
seen the difference that coordinated responses, survivor-centered services, and sustained public awareness can make. However, I also recognize that policy and enforcement alone are not enough. We must confront the beliefs and norms that sustain abuse, and we must create environments where survivors feel safe to speak, seek help, and rebuild their lives without fear of judgment or reprisal.
This book sheds light on the resilience and strength of Nigerian women. Amidst the pain and constraints, there exists a powerful undercurrent of survival, solidarity, and hope. The informal networks of support—friends, neighbors, and community members—serve as lifelines for many women navigating difficult circumstances. These serve to remind us that while systems may fail, human connection often endures.
Importantly, The House Was Never Quiet does not ask us to abandon culture or faith, but rather to critically examine how these forces can both support and constrain women. It calls for a reimagining of traditions in ways that uphold dignity, safety, and equality. It challenges faith communities to move beyond messages of endurance and instead embrace compassion, justice, and protection for the vulnerable.
As you read this book, I encourage you to reflect not only on the stories within its pages but also on the broader questions it raises: What kind of homes are we building? What values are we passing on to the next generation? And how can each of us contribute to creating a society where safety, respect, and dignity are not privileges, but rights?
Tanitoluwa Adeniba, Ph.D.
This work is timely, necessary, and deeply impactful. It is my hope that it will inspire dialogue, inform policy, and, most importantly, encourage action. For in breaking the silence, we take the first step toward change.

PROLOGUE
There is a kind of house that many Nigerian women recognize.
It is not defined by walls or roofs, but by expectations. It is built from proverbs repeated at kitchen tables, sermons delivered with conviction, and advice offered quietly at weddings and naming ceremonies.
Inside this house, girls learn early what is admired and what is corrected. They discover how patience is praised, how sacrifice is expected, and how endurance is framed as strength.
In this house, marriage is considered sacred, sometimes even when it is harmful. Motherhood is honored, even when it requires a woman to pour from an empty cup. Peace is valued, but often the kind that conceals conflicts, rather than ensures safety.
The women who live here are not oblivious. They are negotiating, calculating, and adjusting. Many smile in public and hold their families together with impressive skill. Many fiercely protect their children. And many carry
Tanitoluwa Adeniba, Ph.D.
burdens that remain unseen because they have learned how to bear them quietly.
This book is not only about domestic violence. It’s about the systems that make certain forms of abuse difficult to name and about the tension between love and survival. It’s about how culture, faith, family, and gender expectations shape what women tolerate, what they explain away, and what they endure.
It’s also about motherhood in the middle of all of that. As I examined the data, patterns began to emerge. Patterns of silence, resilience, and stress quietly reshape the emotional landscape of homes. I observed how endurance, when stretched over time, impacts not only women but also the children growing up alongside them. I noticed how survival can maintain a family’s appearance while subtly
straining its relationships.
This is the house many women live in.
And this book is an invitation to open the door and step inside it; not to judge, not to simplify, but to see clearly. To understand what endurance costs, to recognize what resilience hides, and to ask whether survival should be the highest standard we offer mothers and children.
Because what happens inside the house matters.

Let me quickly introduce you to Mariam (not her real name). She was 42 years old, a petty trader and the mother of six children. She lived in Northern Nigeria, far away from her parents and siblings. Mariam was married to a military officer, a man whose uniform commanded respect and influenced how others treated him. To outsiders, he was polite, disciplined, dependable; the kind of man people trusted. But at home, that same need for order manifested
Tanitoluwa Adeniba, Ph.D.
differently. His moods set the tone of the house. His anger arrived without warning.
Mariam learned to read the smallest signs, like how he closed a door or the silence before a question, because her safety depended on it. What the world saw as strength, she experienced as fear.
One night, it happened again. The neighbors heard, but they were used to it by now. And by morning, the house was quiet again. She cleaned before dawn, wiping away as if the events of the night had not happened. The children still had to go to school. Breakfast still had to be made. Life had to keep moving, and she was the one expected to hold everything together, even while she was in pain.
Mariam is not alone. There are many women like her who wake up and keep going, even when their bodies hurt and their hearts feel heavy. Women who clean, cook, and care, not because everything is fine, but because life does not pause for pain. Women who are told to endure, to be patient, to keep the peace, even when peace costs them their safety and well-being.
Whenever Mariam and her husband argued, he would tie her hands behind her back with his belt and beat her in front of the children. Her oldest child begged her to leave. “She said he would kill me one day,” Mariam told me.
But leaving wasn’t easy. Leaving meant starting over with nothing: no money, no support, no certainty. “I was afraid,” she said. “But staying was worse.” And although staying was worse, Mariam didn’t think she had a choice, at least not at the time.

HOW VIOLENCE BECOMES NORMALHOW VIOLENCE BECOMES NORMAL
HOW VIOLENCE BECOMES NORMAL
HOW VIOLENCE BECOMES NORMAL
Like Mariam, many women don’t stay because they’re weak. They stay because they’re trying to keep the home together. Because they’re weighing fear against loss. And because survival often doesn’t look heroic from the outside. It looks quiet, tired, and complicated.
For many women, leaving is not a single decision. It’s a thousand small calculations made every day. Can I endure this a little longer? What will happen to the children if I go? Where will I sleep? Who will believe me? Over time, those questions begin to shape daily life.
What makes stories like Mariam’s hard to sit with is how quickly abuse can become normal. Something that should halt everything gradually slips into everyday life and becomes routine. It blends into how mornings start and nights end, into what is expected, what is avoided, and what is no longer questioned.
At first, abuse feels shocking. Then it becomes confusing, and eventually it starts to seem normal. The body adjusts, and the mind adapts. The line between what is wrong and what feels normal gradually blurs. And violence doesn’t have to happen every day to affect daily life. Sometimes, it’s the anticipation of it that causes the most harm.
This is how abuse persists. Not always through force, but through repetition, silence, and the quiet agreement to get through today and worry about tomorrow later. Until one day, the violence is no longer something that interrupts life. It has become part of the rhythm of the home.
Let’s take a little pause here and ask an important question: What are women taught to recognize as domestic violence in the first place?
In many cultures, including ours, what counts as domestic violence often depends on who is defining it. Most of us were taught to recognize it as bruises, slaps, or broken bones, and yes, those are signs of it.
However, as I spoke with various women, I heard about physical abuse, and then some more. There were stories of insults, threats, humiliation, control over money, restricted movement, isolation from family, constant monitoring, and forced dependence.
Yes, sometimes abuse looks exactly the way we expect it to. It is loud. It leaves marks. It forces explanations. Just like Mariam’s. And even then, many women endure it.
But most of the time, it doesn’t look like what we were taught to watch out for. There are no bruises, broken plates, or raised voices. Instead, it exists in the atmosphere of the house. You sense it before anyone speaks. It appears as the constant effort of adjusting yourself (your tone, your body, your plans) based on someone else’s moods. It manifests as a silent fear that quietly influences everything.
When I began my research, I thought I would mostly hear stories like Mariam’s: physical, visible, and therefore easier to name. I didn’t expect how many women would describe violence that lived quietly in the background of their
lives. Violence that had been explained away, normalized, and endured.
But I didn’t just learn about abuse; I also learned about patterns. Patterns where control gradually appeared and then persisted. Patterns where fear wasn’t caused by a single terrible moment but by many small ones connected over time. Patterns where women adjusted themselves day after day—how they spoke, where they went, how much they asked for—until these adjustments felt normal.
Researchers sometimes describe two patterns that appear in violent relationships.2 In some homes, violence happens when arguments escalate, and things spiral out of control. In others, the pattern is quieter but more consistent. One partner gradually sets the rules of the home, deciding what is allowed, what is questioned, and how much freedom the other person has.
Some women described violence that erupted during arguments. Others described something deeper and more constant: a way of life in which one person’s power shaped everything. And yet, many women didn’t call these experiences violence at all.
In my study of just over a hundred mothers, about eight out of every ten described lives shaped more by control than by isolated physical violence, a pattern that broader reporting suggests is common across many Nigerian households.3
Still, many of them did not label this as abuse at all. This reflects a broader pattern: emotional and psychological abuse are common but seldom recognized. It doesn’t make
headlines and is often brushed off as “marital issues” or “ordinary quarrels.”
Women across different places say similar things. They describe control as jealousy, stress, or as “that’s how he is.” When abuse leaves no physical scars, it becomes easier for others, even women themselves, to downplay it.
One woman told me she has to ask her husband for money for basic things like transportation, food, and sanitary pads. To some, this might seem like structure or leadership. But when a woman needs permission to meet her own daily needs, the situation shifts from care to control.
Another woman spoke about being called “useless” in front of her children. She added quickly, almost defensively, “At least he doesn’t beat me.” That sentence stayed with me. Many women measure suffering against bruises. If there is no blood, no slap, no public scene, then maybe it isn’t real. A woman might be monitored constantly, denied freedom, or humiliated daily, and still believe she hasn’t
been abused.
This is part of what makes domestic violence so hard to identify here. Culture, tradition, and religion often blur the line between abuse and normal behavior. Control is sometimes interpreted as discipline, silence as strength, and endurance as virtue.

One question nagged me: What happens to parenting when violence becomes part of daily life, even when it has no name?
When mothers endure abuse silently, its effects do not stay confined to the marriage. It runs into the home and into the emotional atmosphere that children grow up experiencing. Children living in violent households often absorb tension long before they truly understand it.4
What stood out most in my research wasn’t just the type of violence women experienced. It was the weight they were carrying. The stress, the fatigue, and the constant effort to keep going.
These women still have to show up as mothers despite their fear and exhaustion. They make quiet calculations every day: what to say, what to leave unsaid, how to keep the house calm, and how to shield their children from seeing too much or hearing too much. They carry the weight of violence alongside the responsibilities of motherhood, loving fiercely in spaces that do not feel safe, doing their best with what they have.
Many of these women were functioning but not flourishing. They were doing their best for their children, often from an empty cup. And when something becomes part of the air you breathe, you don’t always notice you’re suffocating.
I know a woman who used to say her house always felt like it was holding its breath. She learned to listen for
her husband’s footsteps before the door even opened. On days when his mood felt heavy, she kept the children close, lowered her voice, turned down the television, and sent them to bed early, not because they were tired, but because she wanted the night to pass quietly.
Later, when the house finally slept, she would sit alone and replay the day in her mind, wondering what she could have done differently, even though she knew the problem was never really her. Her children remember her as calm, gentle, and always present. What they did not see was how much energy it took for her to stay that way.
Parenting under these conditions creates a strain that is rarely acknowledged but deeply felt. It influences how you show up emotionally, mentally, and physically. Over time, it impacts the relationship between mother and child, even when no one talks about it.
As I reviewed the responses from the women in this study, I found myself pausing often. Some stories lingered with me long after I closed my notes. Others were hard to sit with for too long. One thing was clear: the burden these women carried did not vanish once the interviews ended.

Before a woman becomes a wife or a mother, she has already lived in a house that taught her how to survive. This isn’t just the physical house she grew up in, but the emotional one. It’s the atmosphere, the rules, the things that were allowed, and the things that were forbidden.
As I worked on this book, some questions kept popping up in my mind:
Where does a woman learn to stay silent? Where does she learn to endure?
Where does she learn to keep the peace, even when it costs her something?
The answers didn’t begin in marriage. They started in childhood and were rooted in culture.
They influenced how conflicts were managed at home, how anger was expressed or suppressed, whether children were comforted when they cried or told to stop, and whether questions were welcomed or shut down.
Every woman enters marriage with a history. She starts with a nervous system shaped by her upbringing. She enters with beliefs formed by the messages she heard, the love she received, the boundaries she was allowed to have, and the emotions she was permitted to show.
Long before any partner hurts her, a woman already has beliefs about how to respond and adjust. She already knows how to “keep the home”, handle children, and juggle
THE HO US E W A S NEVER Q UIET 
multiple areas of life that are deemed essential to her sense of self and perceived usefulness as a person.

Culture is not the villain.
Nigerian values of loyalty, resilience, communal strength, and respect for elders are powerful and beautiful. They bind families together, create identity, and sustain people through hardship.
But culture becomes distorted when it demands endurance at all costs, when silence is praised as maturity, and when suffering is reframed as strength. The solution is not to discard culture. It is to refine it. We need to:
Keep what nurtures dignity and release what normalizes abuse.

Although this work centers Nigerian mothers, the pattern is global. Across continents, women are told to sacrifice for stability, protect their reputation, keep peace at personal cost, and to endure “for the children.”
The languages differ, but the expectations do not. This is not a Nigerian problem alone; it’s a human one. And the question remains: how many women around the world are living in survival when they were meant to live in fullness?

Living with violence, or its long-term effects, can shape emotional well-being in ways that are not always immediately visible.
A licensed therapist, counselor, or psychologist trained in trauma or family violence can help process experiences safely.
Support groups, whether in person or online, can reduce isolation and help normalize what often feels private.

Help identify options but allow the person to decide what feels safe.

ABOUT THE AUTHOR
Dr. Tanitoluwa Adeniba is a Nigerian-born family scientist, researcher, and writer whose work explores the intersection of culture, motherhood, faith, and family life. She holds a PhD in Human Development and Family Studies, where her research examines domestic violence, parenting stress, and the mother-child relationship among Nigerian women.
Her work is shaped not only by years of research, but by the women she has sat with, listened to, and learned from—especially in the quiet ways abuse is normalized through silence, tradition, and family loyalty.
Writing as both a scholar and a mother, Dr. Adeniba brings compassion, clarity, and deep cultural fluency to conversations most families would rather avoid.
She is the creator of The Family Scientist, a research-informed platform where she translates research into accessible, thought-provoking reflections on relationships, parenting, and breaking harmful patterns.
Through her work, she challenges readers to confront the patterns families inherit and the possibility of choosing differently.
